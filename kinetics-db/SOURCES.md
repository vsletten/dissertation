# Source inventory: mineral dissolution kinetics, 2004–present

This is a **candidate acquisition queue**, not a second rate table. DOI metadata were checked against Crossref on 2026-08-18; inclusion means “worth extracting and reconciling,” not “methodologically interchangeable with P&K.” Foundational pre-2004 kaolinite anchors are retained because the card requires the Ganor/Cama calibration lineage.

P&K parameters are far-from-equilibrium, surface-normalized fits. Later sources often address affinity, reactive-area evolution, flow, anisotropy, secondary coatings, or field scaling; those studies should extend the schema rather than be flattened into a single `log k25` without preserving their experimental contract.

## Baseline and modern compilations

1. **Palandri & Kharaka (2004), _A compilation of rate parameters of water-mineral interaction kinetics for application to geochemical modeling_.** USGS OFR 2004-1068. <https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/2004/1068/pdf/OFR_2004_1068.pdf> — v0 transcription baseline: >70 minerals, three-mechanism Arrhenius parameterization, and explicit limitations.
2. **Heřmanská et al. (2022), _A comprehensive and internally consistent mineral dissolution rate database: Part I: Primary silicate minerals and glasses_.** <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chemgeo.2022.120807> — primary-silicate successor dataset; first overlap set to diff mechanically against P&K.
3. **Heřmanská et al. (2023), _A comprehensive and consistent mineral dissolution rate database: Part II: Secondary silicate minerals_.** <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chemgeo.2023.121632> — secondary silicates/clays; expands kaolinite, smectite, mica, and related phases.
4. **Oelkers et al. (2025), _A comprehensive and consistent mineral dissolution rate database: Part III: Non-silicate minerals including carbonate, sulfate, phosphate, halide, and oxy-hydroxide minerals_.** <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chemgeo.2024.122528> — closes non-silicate scope, but does not replace Parts I–II for this card’s silicate core.
5. **Brantley (2008), _Kinetics of Mineral Dissolution_.** <https://doi.org/10.1007/978-0-387-73563-4_5> — review chapter framing mechanism, affinity, surface area, and lab/field interpretation.
6. **Brantley (2025), _Understanding the Lab-Field Discrepancy in Mineral Dissolution From Flasks to Enhanced Rock Weathering_.** <https://doi.org/10.1029/2025RG000881> — modern synthesis of the scaling gap; useful for schema fields that distinguish intrinsic, effective, and field rates.

## Kaolinite and other clays

7. **Ganor, Mogollón & Lasaga (1995), _The effect of pH on kaolinite dissolution rates and on activation energy_.** <https://doi.org/10.1016/0016-7037(95)00021-Q> — foundational acidic kaolinite activation-energy anchor (~29 kJ/mol in the project survey), intentionally retained despite pre-2004 date.
8. **Cama, Metz & Ganor (2002), _The effect of pH and temperature on kaolinite dissolution rate under acidic conditions_.** <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0016-7037(02)00966-3> — second foundational kaolinite temperature/pH dataset used by the project survey; also pre-2004.
9. **Cama & Ganor (2006), _The effects of organic acids on the dissolution of silicate minerals: A case study of oxalate catalysis of kaolinite dissolution_.** <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gca.2006.01.028> — adds ligand-promoted kinetics and a mechanism P&K’s simple H+/neutral/OH− sum cannot represent.
10. **Yang & Steefel (2008), _Kaolinite dissolution and precipitation kinetics at 22 °C and pH 4_.** <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gca.2007.10.011> — paired dissolution/precipitation constraints near ambient conditions.
11. **Gong, Rimstidt & Zhang (2019), _Unidirectional kaolinite dissolution rates at near-equilibrium and near-neutral pH conditions_.** <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clay.2019.105284> — near-equilibrium affinity behavior absent from far-from-equilibrium v0 records.
12. **Garg & Skibsted (2019), _Dissolution kinetics of calcined kaolinite and montmorillonite in alkaline conditions: Evidence for reactive Al(V) sites_.** <https://doi.org/10.1111/jace.16663> — alkaline/reactive-site data for activated clays; must be tagged as calcined rather than natural mineral.
13. **Kása et al. (2022), _The effect of mechanical and thermal treatments on the dissolution kinetics of kaolinite in alkaline sodium aluminate solution under conditions typical to Bayer desilication_.** <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.clay.2022.106671> — treatment-dependent alkaline kinetics and a warning against mixing industrially activated material with pristine kaolinite.

## Feldspars and silica polymorphs

14. **Harouiya & Oelkers (2004), _An experimental study of the effect of aqueous fluoride on quartz and alkali-feldspar dissolution rates_.** <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chemgeo.2004.01.005> — adds fluoride catalysis for quartz and K-feldspar.
15. **Zhu (2005), _In situ feldspar dissolution rates in an aquifer_.** <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gca.2004.09.005> — direct field-rate constraint for the lab/field bridge.
16. **Beig & Lüttge (2006), _Albite dissolution kinetics as a function of distance from equilibrium: Implications for natural feldspar weathering_.** <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gca.2005.10.035> — albite affinity dependence and near-equilibrium behavior.
17. **Hellmann & Tisserand (2006), _Dissolution kinetics as a function of the Gibbs free energy of reaction: An experimental study based on albite feldspar_.** <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gca.2005.10.007> — independent albite Gibbs-energy/rate relation for reconciling affinity laws.
18. **Sorai, Ohsumi & Ishikawa (2007), _Feldspar dissolution rates measured using phase-shift interferometry: Implications to CO2 underground sequestration_.** <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apgeochem.2007.06.019> — face-resolved interferometric rates and CO2-storage conditions.
19. **Arvidson & Lüttge (2010), _Mineral dissolution kinetics as a function of distance from equilibrium – New experimental results_.** <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chemgeo.2009.06.009> — broader affinity-law tests and rate discontinuity evidence.
20. **Davis, Wesolowski & Rosenqvist (2011), _Solubility and near-equilibrium dissolution rates of quartz in dilute NaCl solutions at 398–473 K under alkaline conditions_.** <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gca.2010.10.023> — high-temperature alkaline quartz data with salinity and near-equilibrium scope.
21. **Yang et al. (2014), _Effects of Al/Si ordering on feldspar dissolution: Part I. Crystallographic control on the stoichiometry of dissolution reaction_.** <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gca.2013.10.047> — ordering/stoichiometry metadata for feldspar records.
22. **Yang et al. (2014), _Effects of Al/Si ordering on feldspar dissolution: Part II. The pH dependence of plagioclases’ dissolution rates_.** <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gca.2013.10.049> — modern plagioclase pH-rate comparison across ordering states.
23. **Pollet-Villard et al. (2016), _Does crystallographic anisotropy prevent the conventional treatment of aqueous mineral reactivity? A case study based on K-feldspar dissolution kinetics_.** <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gca.2016.07.007> — face anisotropy and limits of one bulk surface-normalized constant.
24. **Lange et al. (2021), _The role of crystal heterogeneity in alkali feldspar dissolution kinetics_.** <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gca.2021.06.032> — defect/heterogeneity control for uncertainty and specimen metadata.

## Olivine and enhanced weathering

25. **Velbel (2009), _Dissolution of olivine during natural weathering_.** <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gca.2009.07.024> — natural-weathering morphology and rate constraints.
26. **Köhler, Hartmann & Wolf-Gladrow (2010), _Geoengineering potential of artificially enhanced silicate weathering of olivine_.** <https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1000545107> — early ERW systems analysis; establishes deployment-scale quantities the rate database must serve.
27. **Hausrath & Brantley (2010), _Basalt and olivine dissolution under cold, salty, and acidic conditions_.** <https://doi.org/10.1029/2010JE003610> — low-temperature, saline, acidic end-member kinetics.
28. **Renforth et al. (2015), _The dissolution of olivine added to soil: Implications for enhanced weathering_.** <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apgeochem.2015.05.016> — soil-incubation rates and secondary-product fate.
29. **Montserrat et al. (2017), _Olivine Dissolution in Seawater: Implications for CO2 Sequestration through Enhanced Weathering in Coastal Environments_.** <https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.est.6b05942> — coastal seawater dissolution and practical alkalinity-generation constraints.
30. **Pogge von Strandmann et al. (2022), _The Dissolution of Olivine Added to Soil at 4 °C: Implications for Enhanced Weathering in Cold Regions_.** <https://doi.org/10.3389/fclim.2022.827698> — cold-soil temperature dependence and low-temperature field applicability.
31. **Kahl et al. (2022), _Enhanced weathering in the seabed: Rapid olivine dissolution and iron sulfide formation in submarine volcanic ash_.** <https://doi.org/10.2138/am-2022-8057> — seabed reaction products and coupled Fe/S chemistry.
32. **Flipkens et al. (2023), _Enhanced olivine dissolution in seawater through continuous grain collisions_.** <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gca.2023.09.002> — abrasion/collision enhancement, explicitly separating intrinsic chemistry from physical renewal.
33. **Iff et al. (2024), _The dissolution of olivine added to soil at 32 °C: the fate of weathering products and its implications for enhanced weathering at different temperatures_.** <https://doi.org/10.3389/fclim.2024.1252210> — warm-soil complement to the 4 °C study and product-fate evidence.
34. **Liu et al. (2024), _Estuaries are promising sites for olivine dissolution engineering: Insights from olivine mineralogy_.** <https://doi.org/10.34133/olar.0039> — mineralogy-sensitive estuarine deployment constraints.
35. **Geerts et al. (2025), _Review and syntheses: Ocean alkalinity enhancement and carbon dioxide removal through marine enhanced rock weathering using olivine_.** <https://doi.org/10.5194/bg-22-355-2025> — marine ERW synthesis and a map of unresolved kinetic/verification variables.
36. **Pokharel et al. (2025), _Viability of commercial olivine mixtures for enhanced weathering in seawater: Dissolution kinetics, CO2 sequestration, and metal release assessment_.** <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijggc.2025.104522> — commercial feedstock variability, dissolution, and trace-metal release.

## Wollastonite and serpentine-family carbon removal

37. **Green (2006), _Incongruent dissolution of wollastonite measured with vertical scanning interferometry_.** <https://doi.org/10.2138/am.2006.1968> — face-scale wollastonite rates and incongruence.
38. **Pokrovsky et al. (2009), _Effect of organic ligands and heterotrophic bacteria on wollastonite dissolution kinetics_.** <https://doi.org/10.2475/08.2009.05> — ligand/microbial effects on a key fast-weathering Ca silicate.
39. **Krevor & Lackner (2011), _Enhancing serpentine dissolution kinetics for mineral carbon dioxide sequestration_.** <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijggc.2011.01.006> — pretreatment/process levers for serpentinite carbonation.
40. **Zhao, Park & Lee (2013), _Tuning the dissolution kinetics of wollastonite via chelating agents for CO2 sequestration with integrated synthesis of precipitated calcium carbonates_.** <https://doi.org/10.1039/C3CP52459K> — chelator-promoted wollastonite dissolution coupled to carbonate production.
41. **Salek, Kleerebezem & Jonkers (2013), _Determining the impacts of fermentative bacteria on wollastonite dissolution kinetics_.** <https://doi.org/10.1007/s00253-012-4590-2> — biogenic-acid and microbial process effects.
42. **Critelli et al. (2015), _Dissolution rate of antigorite from a whole-rock experimental study of serpentinite dissolution from 2 < pH < 9 at 25 °C_.** <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apgeochem.2015.06.004> — whole-rock antigorite rate law across broad pH, directly relevant to enhanced serpentinite weathering.
43. **Leonard & Hellmann (2017), _Exploring dynamic surface processes during silicate mineral (wollastonite) dissolution with liquid cell TEM_.** <https://doi.org/10.1111/jmi.12509> — direct nanoscale surface evolution during dissolution.

## Measurement, transport, and lab-to-field scaling

44. **Moore et al. (2012), _Using a reactive transport model to elucidate differences between laboratory and field dissolution rates in regolith_.** <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gca.2012.03.021> — quantitative lab/field reconciliation through transport and regolith state.
45. **Zhu et al. (2016), _Measuring silicate mineral dissolution rates using Si isotope doping_.** <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chemgeo.2016.02.027> — isotope-doping method for low rates and precipitation-interference control.
46. **Jung & Navarre-Sitchler (2018), _Physical heterogeneity control on effective mineral dissolution rates_.** <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gca.2018.02.028> — effective-rate distributions from physical heterogeneity.
47. **Feng et al. (2020), _Hydrodynamic factors influencing mineral dissolution rates_.** <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chemgeo.2020.119578> — flow/hydrodynamic controls that must not be mistaken for intrinsic rate constants.
48. **Li et al. (2020), _The scaling of mineral dissolution rates under complex flow conditions_.** <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gca.2020.01.048> — scale-dependent rates under heterogeneous flow.
49. **Kurganskaya & Lüttge (2021), _Mineral Dissolution Kinetics: Pathways to Equilibrium_.** <https://doi.org/10.1021/acsearthspacechem.1c00017> — modern pathway/affinity framework for moving beyond one far-from-equilibrium law.

## Recommended extraction order

1. Import Heřmanská Parts I–II into a separate versioned source layer and diff overlapping mineral/mechanism identities against P&K.
2. Reconcile kaolinite against Ganor (1995), Cama (2002), Yang & Steefel (2008), and Gong et al. (2019), preserving experimental regime and apparent-vs-mechanistic activation energy.
3. Add face/specimen/area metadata from feldspar anisotropy and heterogeneity studies.
4. Add an `effective_rate_context` extension before ingesting soil, seawater, abrasion, or field studies; those are not direct substitutes for intrinsic BET/geometric-area constants.
5. Keep trace-metal release, secondary phases, and carbon-accounting outputs linked to the rate record rather than squeezing them into the kinetic mechanism table.
